# From a transcript to a discovery draft

Use this reference at intake, when a transcript has gaps, or when accounts conflict. The purpose is a faithful diagram that its participants can understand and revise.

## Establish enough context

Extract the purpose, audience, perspective, trigger, relevant outcomes and exclusions. Capture the owner role and beneficiary when known. Choose `overview` or `operational` detail to fit the reader. Do not ask for facts already established in the material or require every optional context field before modeling.

Check whose perspective the transcript represents. A process owner may know the intended procedure while a participant knows its everyday exceptions. Use `stakeholders` for relevant roles and their coverage: `consulted` with source links, `not-consulted`, or `not-needed` with a scope reason. This is a coverage aid, not an approval hierarchy.

## Ask questions that change the model

Start with a recent concrete instance and follow it from the initiating event to the outcome. For each relevant handoff, establish who sends what, to whom, and how the recipient knows work can begin. Ask about the condition that changes a route, the meaning of completion, and what must be true before an activity starts. Walking backward from the outcome can expose missing prerequisites.

Then ask about a rejected, delayed, canceled, or reworked case if it is material to the scope. “Usually,” “sometimes,” “and,” and “after approval” may hide variants, optional work, uncertain ordering, or an unspecified approver. Do not assume every such word requires a question: ask when the ambiguity affects the intended diagram.

Phrase questions concretely: “Can scheduling begin before the deposit arrives?” Explain the reason plainly: “The transcript mentions both steps but does not say which comes first.” A complete transcript can support several modeling stages in one proposal. Batch related questions without making the user fill in a lengthy questionnaire.

## Distinguish evidence types

Sources may record `origin`, `contributor`, `collectedOn` (YYYY-MM-DD), `period` and `coverage`. Add these only when known. Evidence links may label their `basis` as `reported`, `observed`, `prescribed` or `derived`. The basis belongs to the passage being used; a single interview may describe both policy and actual practice. These labels are independent of current versus proposed state and of claim confidence.

Do not rank source types mechanically. A policy can be current but not followed. An observation can be accurate but cover only one case. A participant can describe an exception rather than the general process. Keep exact source text immutable and place interpretation in claims.

## Reconcile differences visibly

Record competing claims and a `conflict` linking them. Ask whether the difference reflects a time period, customer/case variant, participant perspective, policy versus practice, or an actual disagreement. Resolve it using a cited answer or other evidence; update the affected claims and model in the same proposal. If both accounts apply to distinct variants, model or scope those variants explicitly.

The app checks references and blocks finalization for an open in-scope conflict. It cannot determine which account is true. Marking a conflict resolved does not by itself fix disputed claims or wrong flow. Keep historical sources and revisions.

## Respect the modeling boundary

Do not change the current process because the transcript suggests a better one. Keep improvement suggestions as unmodeled proposed claims if useful. Do not infer durations, probabilities, benefits, or automation requirements. A later request can explore those separately.
