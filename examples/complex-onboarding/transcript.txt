SYNTHETIC TEST INTERVIEW. Brightline Studio, its staff, clients and cases are fictional. These statements are test inputs, not observations of a real business or human approval of a model.
Interviewer: We are documenting how the consultancy currently onboards a signed client, so a new onboarding coordinator can follow the work. We are not designing a better process, setting prices, or describing the delivery project itself. One case is one signed service order. Renewals and later support requests are outside this exercise.
Maya, onboarding coordinator: My case starts when Sales gives me a signed service order and the handover pack. I own the onboarding record and coordinate readiness. Sales has already finished the commercial negotiation; I do not rerun it. The pack normally has the signed order, billing contact, client approver, the list of people who need access, and the agreed scope. Receiving it does not mean it is complete.
Maya: I check that pack before handing work to Finance or Delivery. If something is missing, I send a request back to the client contact and wait for the corrected pack. Then I check it again. Sometimes it goes back twice because the billing address arrived but the access list did not. Once all required items are there, the intake part is complete. There is no automatic timeout for that intake wait in the process we are documenting.
Interviewer: What happens after that? People have mentioned an invoice, a delivery plan and a kickoff booking, and I cannot tell which of those actually holds up which.
Maya: I tell Finance and Delivery that the pack is ready. Finance deals with the deposit, and Delivery talks about dates and the plan. I see those things on the record, but I would want them to confirm whether one is supposed to wait for the other. We certainly do not want the technical engineer building access from a half-agreed plan.
Luis, Finance: A new client normally needs a deposit. I issue the deposit invoice to the billing contact, wait until we can identify the received payment, and record financial clearance. A screenshot saying a transfer was made is not the same as the deposit arriving. We do not include the bank's internal payment processing in our model; we only record our work and the receipt notification.
Luis: The old onboarding checklist says everyone pays a deposit before scheduling. I am not sure that sentence is still safe to use without qualification. We have repeat clients with payment terms, but just being a repeat client is not enough. I need to check exactly what decision we make and when. Please leave that as a question rather than silently removing Finance from those cases.
Nora, delivery lead: Once I can look at the scope, I prepare a delivery plan, send it to the client approver, and wait for their response. Most approve the first version, but occasionally they want a different order of work or different dates. I do not want a diagram that assumes sending the plan means it was approved. I will explain our revision handling when we go through the draft.
Nora: I sometimes pencil a kickoff into the calendar while payment is still outstanding. Maya has seen those calendar entries and reasonably thought the job was booked. There is a difference between holding a possible slot and actually confirming and holding the kickoff. The exact gates matter; let us clarify those instead of treating every calendar entry as the same event.
Interviewer: Could Finance and Delivery both be waiting at the same time? And if one finishes first, does somebody start setup anyway?
Maya: That is exactly the part I want us to agree on together. In our last onboarding I was waiting on both a payment question and a revised plan. I did not start the engineer, but I do not want to turn that one case into a rule without asking the others. Also, we should show whose work it is, rather than one big box that says do onboarding.
Luis: We send a reminder when a deposit has been sitting unpaid. It is not a fresh invoice, and the reminder is not a refusal to accept payment. I remember a seven-day reminder and a fourteen-day cutoff, but I need to confirm when the clocks start, whether those are calendar days, and whether sending the reminder changes the original wait.
Maya: Sometimes the client withdraws while we are waiting for the money or their plan approval. I close the open readiness work so nobody keeps chasing a canceled case. We also release any provisional delivery slot. If we already have a deposit, Finance may need to return it. Please check that policy with Luis; I do not want the cancellation path to forget money we have already received.
Nora: A case can sit with me even when Finance has finished, so any cutoff cannot just be drawn around waiting for payment. If the client never approves the plan, we need to stop my work too. On the other hand, a reminder email should not cancel my plan discussion. Those are different things even though both involve waiting on a client.
Eli, technical setup engineer: I receive the approved details and create the client workspace and accounts. Then I test access for the named users. I have had an incorrect email address make one account fail, so creation alone is not enough. I will give you the exact correction and recheck steps later; I do not want you to guess whether we go straight to kickoff after a failed test.
Eli: The engineer performs the workspace and access checks. Delivery holds the kickoff after access has been verified. We do not describe what the client clicks inside their own systems, and we do not describe the software's internal provisioning algorithms. We care that the right people can get into the workspace and the team is ready to meet them.
Maya: After the kickoff I send the handover pack to Client Success. That is normally the last email I remember sending. I have said done at that point in conversation, but the onboarding record sometimes stays open after the email. We should speak to Client Success about what they need to do before I can close it. I would rather leave the final outcome open than invent their acceptance.
Interviewer: Are Finance, Delivery, technical setup and Client Success independent businesses we exchange formal messages with, or responsibilities inside Brightline?
Maya: They are internal roles in the same consultancy. I coordinate the work across them. The client is the outside participant, and our exchanges with them are real messages. We should keep their internal approval and payment procedure hidden because we have not interviewed anyone about how it works. Sales only supplies the starting pack; its earlier work is outside scope.
Luis: Please distinguish the historical written checklist from what we can confirm people do now. The checklist was useful when we only had new clients. I can check the current rule with the account owner before we finalize the variant. I do not want a note about an exception to become permission to waive a deposit whenever someone is in a hurry.
Nora: The two easy mistakes are calling a tentative calendar hold a completed kickoff, and treating a lack of reply as approval. If the client asks for changes, the current plan is not approved. Our eventual model needs an explicit way back to the plan work and an explicit way out when the case is canceled.
Eli: Please keep the diagram readable. The coordinator should see the overall sequence, and I should be able to open the workspace detail rather than read every Finance step. Small subprocesses are fine provided their inputs, results and responsible roles are clear. They must not hide the unanswered questions about what actually happens.
Maya: For the first draft, show the intake checks we know and the activities we have actually described. Leave the readiness ordering, deadlines, payment-term variant, plan revision loop, access correction and completion gate as questions for the follow-ups. We can discuss normal work first, exceptions second, then walk through a returning-client case and the final handover.
